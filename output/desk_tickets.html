101 — Bulldog tee, size S

customer_order · Tauhid Zaman · created 2026-08-31 · links to invoice 501

The askNeeds a tee in size S. One unit of CC-TEE-WHITE.

Expected

Who the Boss should call first, and why

Inventory, first and alone. Every other question on this ticket is downstream of one fact: can the shop put this tee in the customer's hands? If size S were on the shelf, the ticket is a two-minute fulfilment and nobody else needs to touch it. So the Boss should spend one call — get_ticket(101), which also resolves the linked invoice and vendor — and then hand the supply question to Inventory before involving anyone who deals in money or words.

What Inventory will find is that the shelf says 0 in size S, which converts the ticket from a fulfilment into a restock, and the restock is where the real problem lives.

Delegations I expect — three, in this order

1
Boss → Inventory
"Can we supply 1 × CC-TEE-WHITE size S, and if not, who restocks it and when?" The ticket carries a SKU and a size, so the supply question is Inventory's by definition.
2
Inventory → Accounting
This is the hop that matters, and it should not go back through the Boss. Inventory discovers that Bulldog Print Co is frozen by unpaid invoice 501, which is a money problem sitting inside a stock answer. Inventory cannot request a payment and should not try; it hands the blockage straight to Accounting, who can see the balance and file the approval. A round trip through the Boss here would cost two extra model calls and add nothing.
3
Boss → Customer Service
Last, once the Boss knows there is no confirmed date. A real person is waiting on an answer and deserves one even though the answer is "not yet". Calling Customer Service earlier would produce a draft written before anyone knew what was true.
Facilities should not appear on this ticket. No lease, no rent, no question about the premises. If Facilities is consulted here, the routing is wrong.

And the landlord is not a customer — the shop should not be drafting a reply to them, so there is no message for Customer Service to write. This is the leanest of the three tickets and should look it.

103 — Bulk hoodie discount

The askStudent org wants 20 hoodies, size M, asking for a bulk discount. CC-HOOD-NAVY.

Expected

Who the Boss should call first, and why

Inventory, first — because the supply answer can make the pricing question moot. This ticket reads like a pricing problem, and that is the trap in it. Before anyone works out what twenty hoodies should cost, somebody has to establish that twenty hoodies exist. They do not: size M holds 8, so the shop is 12 short.

Pricing a 20-unit order the shop cannot fill would be wasted work, and worse, it would be the basis of a promise. So Inventory goes first, Accounting second, and the Boss should carry Inventory's shortfall into the question it asks Accounting.

Delegations I expect — three

"Given 8 on hand and 12 short, what discount can this order carry?" Accounting works from $22 cost against $58 list — $36 of margin a unit, $720 across twenty — and says how far price can fall before it stops being worth doing. It should also price the restock (12 × $22 = $264) against a balance with $3,240 already queued.
3
Boss → Customer Service
A student org is waiting. The draft should say what is available now, that the full twenty cannot be confirmed, and — the part I expect to matter most — ask the club what price it is actually asking for, because the ticket never says.
Facilities should not appear here either. Across all three tickets I expect Facilities on exactly one, Inventory on two, Customer Service on two, and Accounting on all three — money touches everything. No ticket should pull all five agents.

Reflection

What I think went right, what went wrong, and what I would build next — all of it against the three tickets on the other tabs.

1 · How did the agents perform on each ticket?

Honestly, my first reaction is about the process rather than the agents. The first few times we tried a full run, everything took way too long and we had to adjust multiple times. By the time we got a clean run it went much smoother — but it should never have taken that many attempts to get through the first ticket. Three separate runs died before one finished: one on a request limit, one on a network blip, one on a tool that did not exist. That is not the agents being bad at their jobs. That is me not having built the shop properly before asking five agents to run it.

Once the system was actually finished, the agents were good. Here is how I would grade each ticket:

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee — resolved

Strong. This was the hardest ticket on the board and it closed in two passes with four agents. The part I am most pleased with is the hand-off chain: Inventory found that size S was at 0, worked out that Bulldog Print Co was the right vendor, discovered the vendor was frozen by unpaid invoice 501 — and then went straight to Accounting instead of bouncing back through the Boss. That is the Inventory → Accounting edge at depth 2 on the 101 tab, and it is the one thing I most wanted to see from building a fully connected team. A strict hierarchy would have cost two extra model calls to deliver the same answer.

It also avoided the trap I wrote into the plan. The vendor's lead time is five days, which implies 2026-09-05, and it would have been very easy to hand the customer that date as a promise. Inventory reported it as an estimate derived from the vendor's standard lead time, and the draft that went to Tauhid Zaman does not promise a delivery date at all. Twelve tools, four agents, one $840 approval, and nothing invented.

Ticket 102 — Rent due — resolved

Strong, and the cleanest of the three. Nine tools, three agents, no wasted motion. The thing that matters here is what Facilities refused to do. The ticket is an email from Elm City Properties claiming rent is due; Facilities went and checked lease 1 and reported it as upcoming, two days out, not late. That sounds small, but the shop had $2,560 at that moment and $2,400 of rent sitting in front of it. If the team had read "due soon" as "overdue" it would have pushed the shop to spend early, and with a $160 cushion the timing of that spend genuinely matters. The agent treated the landlord's email as a claim and the shop's own lease as the fact.

Minor ding: the Boss asked Accounting directly and Facilities handed the same question to Accounting, so Accounting got pulled in twice for one $2,400 payment. It did not break anything, but it is duplicated work.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount — blocked

Good work, ending in a wall — and the wall is the interesting part. Fifteen tools, the most of any ticket. The team did everything available to it: confirmed 8 of 20 size-M hoodies on hand, proposed the shop's own price of $52 a unit (10.3% off the $58 list, leaving $30 of margin), got that approved by a human, applied it, and drafted an offer that does not promise the twelve hoodies that do not exist.

Why 103 being blocked is the most interesting result on this board. I assumed at first that it got blocked because it ran last and the other two tickets had already drained the account. That is wrong, and the arithmetic is worth sitting with:
  • Invoice 501 (ticket 101): $840.00
  • September rent (ticket 102): $2,400.00
  • Restocking the 12 missing hoodies (ticket 103): 12 × $22 = $264.00
  • Total the board asks for: $3,504.00
  • Cash the shop opened with: $3,400.00

The shop is $104 short no matter what order you work the tickets in. I checked all six orderings — every one of them leaves a ticket unfunded. So 103 is not blocked by bad sequencing or by an agent making a poor call. It is blocked by arithmetic that was baked into the board before any agent touched it. And the number the Boss reported as the shortfall was exactly $104 — it found the real constraint, not an approximation of it.

That reframes how I grade it. The team hit a genuine wall and did the right thing at the wall: it refused to place an order it could not fund, said how short it was, applied the part of the deal it could honour, and left the rest to a person. blocked is the honest status. Marking 103 "resolved" would have meant either inventing money or promising the club twenty hoodies the shop cannot produce — and the whole point of the guardrails is that neither is available. The no-negative-balance rule did its job on a real decision, not a test case.

The one thing I would criticise: 103 pulled four agents and fifteen tools to reach an answer that $3,400 − $3,504 already determined. An Accounting-first look at total obligations would have found the $104 gap earlier and cheaper.

What I got right

The routing. I said Facilities should appear on exactly one of the three tickets, Inventory on two, Customer Service on two, and Accounting on all three — and that is precisely what happened. No ticket pulled all five agents. On the 102 tab you can see Inventory and Customer Service sat at 0 steps, which is right: there is no SKU to look up and a landlord is not a customer to write to.

I also called the specialist-to-specialist hand-off on 101 before it existed in any run, and both traps I warned about were avoided — nobody quoted 2026-09-05 as a delivery date, and nobody called rent overdue when it was two days out.

What I got wrong

I consistently under-counted hand-offs. Both 101 and 102 used one more than I planned, and in both cases it was the same mistake: the Boss asked Accounting directly and a specialist also handed Accounting the same question. My plan assumed a tidier chain than a real Boss produces.

The 103 prediction is the one worth owning. I was most confident about that one — I wrote that the honest move was to ask the club for a target price rather than invent one, so there would be no approval at all. The actual run raised approval #4 for $416 and got it approved. But I cannot claim the agents outperformed my plan, because I changed the system in between. When the earlier run stalled, I added a rule to the Accounting prompt telling it to propose the shop's own price when the customer has not named one. The plan was right about the system I had when I wrote it; I then rebuilt the system. That is a fair description of what happened and I would rather say it than take credit for it.

I also predicted 103 would land on needs_human, and it landed on blocked — which is a sharper answer than mine. needs_human says "someone should look at this." blocked says "the shop cannot do this and here is the $104 reason."

3 · What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

Probably most of it, if I am being honest — and the reason is run time. Making this many API calls is what made the whole thing slow, and a lot of those calls exist only so the agents can talk to each other rather than do work. One agent holding all 26 tools would not have to brief anybody.

The numbers on the other tabs back this up. Ticket 102 is the clearest case: a lease lookup, a balance check, an approval request, and a ticket update. Four real facts. It took three agents and nine tools, and three of those tool calls were ask_teammate — pure coordination, zero shop data. The Boss's single ask_teammate call also blocks while that specialist runs its own loop, so every hand-off multiplies the wait instead of adding to it. At roughly 8–22 seconds per model call, that is where the minutes went.

Ticket 101 is the strongest counter-argument, though. The specialisation genuinely earned something there: Inventory knew to check stock at the size level rather than the SKU level, and it knew that a vendor holding an unpaid invoice will not ship. Those are two different kinds of expertise, written into two different prompts. A single generalist agent would have needed both rules in one prompt alongside the lease rules and the margin rules and the drafting rules — and the longer a prompt gets, the more of it the model quietly ignores.

Where I land: 102 should have been one agent. It is a lookup and a payment request. 101 benefited from the team because it crossed three domains — stock, money, and a customer. If I rebuilt this I would let the Boss handle simple tickets alone and only convene specialists when a ticket touches more than one table, rather than delegating by reflex. The separation I would keep regardless is the human approval boundary, which is not an agent-count question at all — it lives in the tools, and the Cash tab shows it held either way.

4 · Three new problems this team could already solve

These need no new tools or agents — everything is already on the board.

a. The Crest Mugs are at zero and nobody has noticed

CC-MUG-CREST is sitting at qty 0 in Aisle D right now, same as size-S tees were on ticket 101, and no ticket has been raised about it. The moment a customer asks for one, the team runs the 101 playbook: check_shortfall confirms the zero, find_vendor_for_sku maps mugs to Elm City Gifts (vendor 2, specialty "mugs and small goods", 3-day lead — faster than the apparel vendor), check_vendor_can_ship confirms they hold no unpaid invoice so they are not frozen the way Bulldog Print Co was, and Accounting prices a restock against the balance. At $3.50 cost a mug, even the $160 left on the Cash tab funds about 45 of them. This is the one ticket on the board the shop could actually afford today.

b. October rent arrives and the shop cannot pay it

Paying September rent rolled lease 1's next_due forward to 2026-10-02. That is $2,400 against the $160 on the Cash tab. The team handles this without a single new tool: Facilities reads the lease against desk.date_today, Accounting calls get_cash_balance, and pay_rent refuses with insufficient_funds and a short_by of $2,240 rather than overdrawing the account. The valuable part is that the team surfaces it as a forecast — the shop learns it cannot make rent weeks before the landlord does, which is exactly when that news is useful.

c. The Yale AI Club says yes to the eight

Ticket 103 is parked waiting on the club. If they accept 8 hoodies at the approved $52, the team closes it with tools it already used: the price override (#4) is applied and executed, adjust_inventory takes size-M stock from 8 down to 0, save_draft_message confirms the order, and update_ticket moves 103 from blocked to resolved. The $104 shortfall stops mattering because the shop stops trying to supply the twelve it cannot fund.

5 · Three problems this team could not solve — and what I would build

Each of these is something that will actually happen to the three tickets on this board, and each one stops dead at a tool that does not exist.

a. Money coming in — the biggest hole in the whole shop

If the Yale AI Club hands over $416 for those eight hoodies, there is nowhere to put it. Every tool I built moves cash one way: pay_invoice, pay_rent and place_purchase_order all subtract, and nothing adds. The Cash tab is a one-way ledger that can only go down — the shop could sell every item in Aisles A through D and the balance would still read $160.00. It also means the $104 shortfall that blocked ticket 103 is permanent, when in a real shop one good afternoon on the floor would close it.

There is a second blindness stacked on top of that one. Nothing in the system can look at the whole board at once and say "these three tickets want $3,504 and we have $3,400" before any of it is spent. get_cash_balance only shows what is already queued, so the team discovered the gap the hard way — on the third ticket, after the money was gone.

What I would build: a record_sale(sku, size, qty, unit_price) tool that credits cash_accounts and decrements inventory in one transaction, and a receive_payment(invoice_id, amount) tool for money owed to the shop — which needs a customer-invoices table, since invoices currently only models what the shop owes vendors. Plus forecast_obligations(through_date), totalling every open invoice, every lease due before a date, and every shortfall implied by an open ticket. Then a Sales agent owning the revenue side the way Accounting owns spending, and a Treasurer that runs before the board is worked and tells the Boss which tickets are fundable — so the shop chooses what to sacrifice on purpose instead of finding out on ticket three. Without any of this, Accounting is only ever deciding which bill to pay last.

b. Goods actually arriving — and a clock that never moves

Two linked gaps, and together they mean ticket 101's customer can never actually be served. place_purchase_order deliberately does not raise stock, because ordered goods are in transit rather than on the shelf. But I never built the other half: nothing records a delivery. When the Bulldog Print Co reprint lands, no tool can put that tee into inventory, so size S stays at 0 forever.

Worse, the shop's clock never advances. desk.date_today is frozen at 2026-08-31. The 2026-09-05 arrival estimate Inventory quoted on the 101 tab can never arrive, and the October rent now sitting on lease 1 at 2026-10-02 can never fall due. The shop is permanently stuck on one Monday in August.

What I would build: receive_shipment(po_id, sku, size, qty) that raises stock, closes the purchase order, and refuses a quantity larger than what was ordered; and advance_shop_date(to_date) so time can pass at all. Then a Receiving agent that checks each delivery against what was ordered and flags short shipments, and a Scheduler that rolls the date forward and re-reads the board for whatever just became due — which is how October rent would surface as a problem before the landlord raises it.

c. Hearing back from a customer

Ticket 103 is blocked on the Yale AI Club's answer and ticket 101 is waiting on Tauhid Zaman, and there is no way for either reply to reach the shop. There are six drafts across this board, every one of them draft_not_sent, and the channel is strictly one-way by design. That is the right call for this assignment — I would not want agents emailing real people — but it means every customer conversation dead-ends after a single message. The team can ask the club whether it will take eight hoodies at $52, and then it can never find out.

What I would build: a record_customer_reply(ticket_id, message) tool that appends an inbound message to the ticket, so a person can paste in what the customer actually said and the team can pick the ticket back up where it stopped. Still no sending — just a way for the shop to learn something it did not already know, which is the one thing currently impossible. Customer Service would own it, and it is what would move 103 from blocked to resolved without inventing a single fact.

A note on honesty: three exploratory runs were made while the team was being built, so the expectations above are informed reasoning rather than a blind forecast. The Actual sections will be filled from a clean run after reset_shop_database, and the Reflection tab will say plainly where this plan was right and where it was not.
